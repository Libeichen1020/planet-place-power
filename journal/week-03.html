Pattern, Scale and Density, New York City’s Terrain and Shape

The question

What soil properties and terrain patterns are visible at my site in New York City, and how might they shape water movement?

The data

Elevation and slope: I used Esri’s Terrain layer, a global elevation service that returns ground-height values in metres and compiles data from multiple providers at different resolutions. At my site, the World Elevation Coverage Map identified USGS CoNED TBDEM (USGS_CoNED_TBDEM1m) as the finest available dataset, with a 1 m pixel size. Its LE90 is not stated; the vertical datum is listed as variable, and the date range is 1859–2020. The 1859 start date is a metadata artifact, not an actual survey date. I also used Terrain – Slope Degrees, which derives slope dynamically from Terrain using a server-side function. Soil: I used SoilGrids to examine organic carbon density at the 15–30 cm depth interval. SoilGrids’ original maps are available under CC BY 4.0. I also added Esri’s World Soils 250m Soil Organic Carbon layer, whose item page lists the Esri Master License Agreement. All items require an ArcGIS Online organizational subscription or an ArcGIS Developer account and does not consume credits.

The method

I used ArcGIS Map Viewer to display Terrain – Slope Degrees for my site. I made two renderings of the same slope data: First, I kept the natural breaks. Secondly, I narrowed the colour ramp’s data range and classified the layer into five classes using manual breaks as shown in the screenshot below. For the classified rendering, I used five manually defined classes: 0–5°, 5–10°, 10-15°, 15–30°, and 30–90° to increase the visual contrast. I kept the map extent and zoom the same so the two renderings could be compared. The elevation values were converted to slope dynamically by the Terrain service. For the soil map, I selected organic carbon density and the 15–30 cm depth interval in SoilGrids. I also viewed the World Soils 250m Soil Organic Carbon layer in ArcGIS as a republication of the SoilGrids data, and switched on its uncertainty display to compare uncertainty across the area.

The evidence

What I found

Expectation: I expected my New York City site to be mostly flat becuase it is mostly covered by human-made surface. For rain water within the city, I expected it to flow toward the nearest lower ground, such as a street drain. For natural water body such as Hudson River, I expected it to flow from the North to the south and eventually merges into the ocean. I would be surprised if the map showed a steep slope or major ridge at New York City because I expected the local terrain to be relatively flat.

Regional: Across the New York City region, the map shows mostly low-slope terrain, with steeper bands in some nearby areas such as Jersey City and Staten Island. The relief around the region’s coastline explain this contrast: inland areas are steeper, while low-lying coastal areas are flatter. After zooming out into a greater region, the pattern becomes more straightforward.

Site: At my site, Whashington Square Park, becuase the ground is mainly covered by the humman made surface, there is no clear direction that where the surface water would go. The loose material would be carried by the surface runoff and collect in the street drain.

Comparison: My site appears flatter than the surrounding region because of its highly human-made surface, which makes my site an outlier. Its elevation data are unusually fine relative to many sites in the class: the finest dataset identified here is USGS CoNED TBDEM 1 m, while several sites in the tutorial’s comparison use coarser elevation data. So this map can show smaller terrain variations than those coarser datasets. For my New York City site to be in the 1 m group, a public agency—such as USGS would need to commission a 1 m elevation survey, likely using LiDAR. The agency would then need to publish the data openly so it could be accessed by Arcgis.

What this does not show

This map cannot show exactly where rainwater will flow through my site or where it will collect because the map does not show drainage infrastructure, buildings, curbs, or other features that can redirect water. Also, this map cannot show features smaller than 1-meter cell size given the resolution.
