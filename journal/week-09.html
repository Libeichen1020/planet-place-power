Week 09 · November 2026

From Maps to a Story Map

The question

Potential outdoor heat exposure in New York City varies with mapping scale, spatial unit, and resolution：How does the pattern and distribution of potential heat exposure change as the mapping unit shifts from a weather station to terrain cells, multispectral imagery, and classified land cover？

The data

The following audit covers all seven maps and mapped products created between Weeks 2 and 8. It records the unit used by each map, whether the material can be republished, whether I traced it to its origin, and whether it contributes directly to the StoryMap’s claim.

Week 2 — Climate. One monitoring point: Central Park weather station, identified by GHCN-D station ID USW00094728. The station stands in for broader climate conditions across New York City. The observations were traced to NOAA’s GHCN-D archive and NOAA-produced data are generally in the U.S. public domain, with attribution retained.

Week 3 — Terrain. USGS CoNED topobathymetric elevation represented by 1 m raster cells across the displayed New York City extent. The cell size determines which small variations in the physical surface remain visible. The data were traced to the USGS CoNED program and USGS-produced data are generally in the U.S. public domain.

Week 4 — Vegetation. Landsat multispectral imagery represented by 30 m raster cells, with Washington Square Park defining the study area. The Agriculture rendering uses spectral reflectance, with brighter green indicating more vigorous vegetation. The imagery was traced to USGS and NASA Landsat data hosted through AWS and served through Esri Living Atlas. Use is governed by the Esri Master License Agreement.

Week 5 — Watershed. A delineated catchment derived from MERIT Hydro and an explicitly placed pour point. The higher-precision delineation produced an approximately 2 km² catchment, with an outlet near 40.741, −74.008. The data were traced to MERIT Hydro. MERIT Hydro is available under CC BY-NC 4.0 or ODbL 1.0; derived database products may carry additional share-alike requirements.

Week 6 — Land cover. Esri and Impact Observatory Sentinel-2 land cover represented by 10 m raster cells within the extent displayed on screen around Washington Square Park and NYU’s Paulson Center. Each cell is assigned to a fixed land-cover class. The product was traced to the Sentinel-2 10m Land Use/Land Cover Time Series and is available under CC BY 4.0.

Week 7 — Table to map. Natural Earth country polygons used as the target layer and country names used as the join key. Seven polygons lacked matching trade rows: Western Sahara, United States of America, Puerto Rico, Antarctica, Northern Cyprus, Somaliland, and Kosovo. Every row in the trade table matched a Natural Earth polygon. Natural Earth boundaries are in the public domain. The Usage and Citation statement says that the Atlas is a freely available public resource and that reuse and adaptation are encouraged when users appropriately cite the Atlas and its underlying data sources. The files were based on Comtrade data last retrieved on December 15, 2025.

Week 8 — Infrastructure. An on-screen bounding box covering New York City’s five boroughs, Jersey City, and part of Long Island. The infrastructure count depends on the chosen query extent rather than a single administrative boundary. Overpass Turbo data was made by OpenStreetMap contributors and is licensed under the Open Database License (ODbL) 1.0. References and screenshots of the TeleGeography’s Submarine Cable Map are available under CC BY-SA 4.0 when TeleGeography is credited. The license for Data Centers map is not explicitly stated.

The method

I first audited all seven maps according to their mapping unit, spatial extent, source, and republication conditions. I then applied one selection question to each map: Does this map change what a reader believes about potential outdoor heat exposure in New York City?

I retained the climate, terrain, vegetation, and land-cover maps. I excluded the watershed map because it primarily explains drainage connectivity, the trade map because it operates at a global country scale, and the infrastructure map because it represents telecommunications rather than environmental exposure. These excluded maps remain in the journal as part of the working record, even though they do not belong in this particular argument.

I organized the four selected maps as a scale ladder: 1. Central Park weather station; 2. 1 m terrain; 3. 30 m multispectral vegetation imagery; 4. 10 m classified land cover. The first map establishes broad climate conditions through a single monitoring point. The second changes the unit from one point to continuous 1 m raster cells, showing physical variation that the climate record cannot represent. The third changes from 1 m terrain cells to 30 m spectral cells within a park-centered study area, introducing vegetation while reducing spatial detail. The fourth moves from spectral vegetation signals to fixed 10 m land-cover classes across the displayed extent, showing how classification determines whether physical change becomes visible.

Each map was added with connective text explaining why it occupies that position. The first three were embedded from my ArcGIS Online Web Map with different layer visibility and initial extents. The final land-cover comparison was embedded from the Esri Sentinel-2 Land Cover Explorer in swipe mode so readers can interactively compare 2017 and 2025.

The evidence

Open the StoryMap

What I found

Viewed together, these maps show that potential outdoor heat exposure becomes more spatially specific as the mapping unit changes from a single weather station to 1 m terrain cells, 30 m multispectral imagery, and 10 m land-cover classes. The climate record indicates broader thermal conditions, while the terrain, vegetation, and land-cover maps reveal local physical conditions that may increase or reduce exposure during walking.

The sequence also shows that the visible pattern depends on resolution, boundary, and classification. For example, the Paulson Center underwent substantial physical redevelopment but remains classified as Built Area in both 2017 and 2025. A physical change can therefore remain invisible when it does not produce a change between the dataset’s predefined land-cover classes. No individual map captures both atmospheric heat and the local protection available to pedestrians, but their sequence reveals why a citywide weather observation alone cannot represent outdoor walking conditions.

What this does not show

The maps do not directly measure pedestrian thermal comfort, route-level shade, or air temperature around individual subway entrances. The vegetation map uses 30 m Landsat cells and cannot identify individual tree canopies, while the land-cover map classifies 10 m cells without representing building height, street geometry, or time-specific building shade.

What is missing is an integrated map combining station-specific heat conditions, tree canopy and building shade within subway walking catchments, and station-level ridership. Without that map and corresponding statistical analysis, this StoryMap cannot determine whether outdoor walking heat exposure is associated with subway ridership.
